## Executive verdict

The report should be treated as `needs review` for four independent reasons:

3. candidate-facing rewrites are corrupt and not actual rewritten answers;
4. completion, evidence totals, visible turn count, and source coverage cannot be reconciled from the PDF.

## Detailed findings

### 1. `Use of real examples: 0 turns` is wrong

The report contains at least these direct examples:

| Turn | Direct evidence |
|---|---|
| Q3 | Voice interview latency began around 12 seconds; the candidate traced frontend, backend, STT, answer understanding, question generation, and Azure Speech; latency ended around 3 seconds. |
| Q4 | The candidate describes ownership in a food recommendation application and sharing prompt/test-driven workflow practices with a teammate. The outcome is weak, but the project is still real. |
| Q6 | The candidate describes a previous-role experiment with engineers, operators, and other teams, 40–50 units, and a retest-rate reduction. |

Root cause: `classifyEvidenceType` checks `I can`, `I could`, and `I would` before completed-work evidence. Q6 contains `I can bring this ... into this role`, so a future transfer statement overrides the completed experiment. The direct-evidence regex also misses ordinary phrases such as `I tried`, `I did`, `I shared`, and `in my previous role`.

Correct interpretation:

> You used several real examples, including measurable results. The main weakness was structure: actions, validation, trade-offs, and outcomes were not always stated cleanly.

### 2. `Theoretical answers: 8` and `Generic answers: 7` are not credible categories

Those totals sum to 15 and leave no direct or adjacent experience despite the examples above. The classifier forces each raw user turn into a single category and lets a hypothetical phrase erase past evidence. It also analyses all raw user turns rather than the report’s paired accepted answers.

The categories should be:

- direct-example present;
- hypothetical-only;
- mixed past evidence plus future transfer;
- generic/no usable evidence.

Only hypothetical-only should count as theoretical.

### 3. Q2 uses the wrong rubric

Question:

> Can you walk me through a specific example of how you validated that the feedback it gave actually helped a candidate improve?

Displayed dimensions:

> Motivation 5/10 | Company Reason 0/10 | Role Reason 0/10 | Candidate Evidence 10/10 | Specificity 10/10

These dimensions do not answer the question. A valid rubric needs validation method, participant or user evidence, before/after comparison, outcome, limitations, and next step.

Root cause: follow-ups inherit `questionFamily` and `evidenceMode` from the parent question. Report rubric inference then trusts stale motivation metadata before the actual validation wording. A previous safeguard that preferred the spoken question was effectively regressed by the later role-framework change.

The candidate’s answer is still weak: it explains the report framework but does not provide a real candidate-improvement validation example. A reasonable directional score is around 3–4/10, but the PDF’s dimension scores are invalid and should not be reused.

### 4. Q3 Action 0/10 is a deterministic false negative

The answer includes actions:

- used console logging;
- traced frontend, backend, STT, and Azure Speech stages;
- identified answer-understanding and question-generation delays;
- changed the intermediate understanding/routing approach.

Root cause: the action vocabulary recognises selected verbs such as `implemented`, `tested`, and `debugged`, but not the noisy transcript’s `tried`, `using`, `found`, `realise`, and `transfer/change` wording.

`Situation 10 | Action 0 | Result 10` is internally implausible. The answer is disorganised, but Action should be at least partial. The supplied estimate of roughly 6–7/10 overall is defensible.

### 5. Q4’s 8/10 is inflated

The ownership story is relevant, but the outcome is essentially:

> Our application was really great.

That does not justify Result 10/10. Reflection 10/10 is also unsupported; the answer describes learning resources and making a teammate’s coding better, not a clear first-person lesson.

Root causes:

- an unquantified mention of `user` supplies a result-unit point;
- `feedback` supplies an outcome point even when it describes a system feature;
- any `learn` plus `better` language can produce full reflection credit.

A more defensible score is roughly 6–7/10, with useful ownership/action evidence and a weak result.

### 6. Q5 is scored as if it were a standalone full STARR answer

Q5 asks only for the outcome of the Q4 story. The report gives zeroes for Task and Action because the candidate does not repeat the whole story. That is an assessment-design error: targeted follow-ups should score the requested dimension and use the parent story as context.

The candidate’s outcome remains too vague, but the report should say `Scored focus: Result/Reaction`, not penalise missing restatement.

### 7. Q6 is the strongest answer, but its result is under-scored

The answer contains:

- previous-role context;
- cross-functional collaboration;
- process learning from operators and engineers;
- experiment design;
- 40–50 tested units;
- a percentage reduction in retest rate;
- a transfer statement about consulting non-technical clinic stakeholders.

Root cause of Result 5/10: the result-unit regex wraps `%` in a trailing word boundary. A percentage symbol is not a word character, so `15%`/`50%` does not earn that point. The digit earns only the first point.

Reflection 0/10 is also arguably too harsh. The closing transfer statement is not a polished lesson, but it is at least a partial application/reflection signal.

### 8. The report ignores an internal numeric conflict

Q1 says the retest rate moved from 15% to 5%. The Q6 transcript printed in the PDF says 50% to 5%. The supplied critique assumes 15% is correct, and the same-session export also contains the noisy 50% variant.

This should not be silently normalised. The report should preserve raw text, flag the conflict, and ask for confirmation. Until confirmed, the result can be recognised as quantitative but should carry reduced confidence.

### 9. Q7 uses the wrong assessment shape

Question:

> What tradeoffs or constraints did you have to consider when designing your experiments?

The report applies full STARR and gives Action/Result/Reflection zeroes. The question asks for constraints and decision quality. The correct dimensions are requirements, options, trade-offs, risk/quality controls, validation, and expected or observed outcome.

The answer is difficult to understand because of ASR quality, but it mentions process/material issues, operator workflow, test-item reproducibility, fixture changes, and stopping conditions. Those signals should be evaluated under a trade-off rubric rather than discarded through STARR.

### 10. Q8’s score is likely inflated

The curiosity answer describes ongoing meetup participation, AI communities, new technology, web coding, and TDD. It does not clearly describe one bounded event despite `Tell me about a time`.

`Situation 10/10` is driven partly by answer length and generic context words. `Reflection 10/10` is driven by `learned` and `better`; `Result 5/10` is driven by generic improvement language. A score near 4–5/10 is more defensible than 6/10 unless the answer is reframed around one specific learning event and resulting application.

### 11. The evidence snapshot contradicts the top score cards

Top cards:

- Overall: 58.60
- CV-JD match: 64.30
- Interview: 53.00

Evidence Snapshot:

- Overall role fit: 64.30

Root cause: `buildPlainEnglishMetrics` uses `analysisResult.overallScore`, which is the CV-JD match score, before the draft computes the blended report score. The same concept therefore displays two values.

The snapshot should show Overall 58.60 and a separate CV-JD metric 64.30.

### 12. All aggregate report scores are downstream of flawed turn scoring

The 53 interview score and 58.6 blended score are computed from framework breakdowns that include the wrong Q2 rubric and heuristic false positives/negatives. The arithmetic may be internally correct, but the inputs are not. These values must be regenerated after rubric and evidence fixes; they should not be manually patched in the PDF.

### 13. Strength presentation is not auditable

`What You Did Well` lists JavaScript, Python, SQL, and Documentation with repeated generic copy. The visible transcript clearly supports JavaScript, but the PDF does not show why Python, SQL, or Documentation are strengths. They may come from CV/JD analysis, which is legitimate, but the section does not distinguish CV evidence from interview evidence.

The same report later says there is limited direct evidence for basic programming, which can appear contradictory. Each strength needs a source and snippet; normalised strength/gap labels need a contradiction check.

### 14. Evidence strength 1.20/4 is under-sensitive and poorly explained

The evidence-strength rules miss seconds, noisy action verbs, and several result phrasings. They also do not show which of context/action/validation/outcome was detected per answer. A low aggregate may be justified because many answers are unclear, but 1.20 cannot be trusted under the current signal extractor.

### 15. Completion cannot be trusted from the PDF alone

The report says `15/15 answered`, while turn-by-turn feedback shows Q1-Q8.

Two separate issues exist:

1. The PDF code explicitly renders only the first eight turn breakdowns. This is confirmed.
2. Backend completion uses raw role counts and a loose question detector instead of the explicit turn-counting contract. This can count repair/confirmation turns. This is confirmed in code.

What is not proven: the stored report may genuinely contain 15 accepted answers. Evidence diagnostic totals sum to 15, which suggests the report analysed 15 user turns. The local transcript export ends at the Q7 question and was created before the PDF; it is not a complete final-session source. Therefore the exact stored 15-turn composition remains unverified without the protected persisted report artifact.

Required fix: use one canonical accepted question-answer dataset and export every scored turn.

### 16. Stronger versions are corrupt for two separate reasons

The backend deterministic fallback emits Chinese bracket prompts such as `[補充情境]` and `[說明核心原則]`. jsPDF uses a standard font that cannot encode them, producing mojibake such as `Šª...`.

Even if the font rendered correctly, these are structural placeholders, not stronger answers. The section is mislabeled and unusable.

The QA layer checks only that an answer-rewrite array is non-empty. It does not check readability, placeholders, language, relationship to the question, grounding, or length.

Correct behaviour:

- show a real grounded English rewrite of at most 120 words; or
- hide the invalid content and show that a reliable stronger answer could not be generated.

### 17. The evidence appendix is not evidence

The appendix shows one `Role match evidence` card and repeated `Job requirement` cards. It does not show a claim, requirement text, answer number, transcript snippet, or confidence.

Root causes:

- the PDF ignores existing `claimText` and `evidenceSnippets` fields;
- generic analysis/retrieval references appear before grounded claim references;
- the PDF slices the first eight references;
- QA rewards reference count even if references are duplicates without content.

A useful row is:

| Claim | Source | Evidence snippet | Confidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| Latency debugging | Answer Q3 | “around 12 seconds ... around 3 seconds” | Medium; ASR affected |
| Cross-functional experiment | Answer Q6 | “40 to 50 units ... retest rate ... to 5%” | Medium; starting percentage needs confirmation |

### 18. `Coverage score 83/100` creates false confidence

Coverage rewards section count, raw evidence-reference count, question-count ratio, and average evidence strength. Duplicate empty references and questionable turn counts can therefore create a high score. It does not detect the corrupt rewrite or Q2 rubric mismatch.

Coverage should count unique claim-level references with snippets and must not override blocking integrity flags.

### 19. `Grounding risk: Low To Medium` understates visible defects

Current QA risk is driven by a small flag list. It lacks checks for:

- rubric-question mismatch;
- evidence-total mismatch;
- score-label mismatch;
- rewrite readability;
- duplicate source references;
- omitted exported turns;
- transcript conflicts.

Until those checks pass, the report should remain `Needs review` with visible reasons.

### 20. ASR quality affects scoring but is not explained to the candidate

Examples include:

- `Ellen` instead of the canonical candidate name;
- `CB and Java description` instead of CV and job description;
- `by coding` instead of vibe coding;
- `proper engineering` instead of prompt engineering;
- `text driven` instead of test-driven;
- `sleep situation task...` instead of STAR/STARR;
- 15% versus 50%.

The application already has phrase hints, transcript normalisation, and low-confidence understanding confirmation. However, session phrase hints omit the candidate name, the safe replacement list is narrow, and raw/normalised segment provenance is lost before report persistence.

The fix must remain conservative: improve phrase hints and preserve corrections, but never rewrite names or numbers silently.

### 21. Voice-delivery metrics lack interpretation

`38 filler words`, `15 long pauses`, and `106.33 WPM` may be valid measured values, but the report does not state:

- number or duration of analysed turns;
- definition of a long pause;
- thresholds for concern;
- whether ASR/VAD segmentation affected the count.

The red warning style is stronger than the evidence explanation. Add capture coverage and limitations before presenting this as a negative signal.
